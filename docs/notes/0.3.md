# 0.3 Daisy: notes

What we tried on the way to colonies, what the numbers said, and what changed because of them. Kept as we go, so the dead ends stay in. Scores are from `terrarium.evals` (64 episodes, seed 1,000,000) unless a line says otherwise; "the 0.2 brain" is `17cc35ad7932`.

## The 0.2 brain in a colony, untrained

Before writing any colony code: drop the solo forager into arenas with 6 or 12 Terrikins and see what it does. It had never seen another creature.

| | random | greedy | 0.2 brain |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6, 8 food | 0.22 / 49.4 | 5.45 / 115.0 | 6.34 / 134.8 |
| 6, 24 | 0.65 / 61.6 | 16.21 / 175.4 | 17.77 / 179.4 |
| 6, 48 | 1.51 / 86.0 | 30.66 / 180.0 | 32.26 / 180.0 |
| 12, 8 | 0.18 / 47.9 | 3.50 / 87.5 | 3.74 / 90.4 |
| 12, 24 | 0.59 / 59.6 | 10.20 / 162.7 | 10.65 / 166.0 |
| 12, 48 | 1.15 / 74.5 | 19.55 / 179.5 | 20.23 / 179.6 |

Food a minute / seconds alive.

- It carries over: at or above greedy everywhere. Its lead shrinks as it gets crowded, from a third better than greedy alone to 7% with 12 creatures on 8 food.
- With 8 food regrowing every 6–12 s, an arena makes about 53 food a minute and 12 Terrikins eat about 45, so supply decides, not skill. Carrying food home only means something with a surplus, so food counts for 0.3 get picked once there's a carrying baseline.

## Faster rays: not needed yet

Rays were the obvious worry: every ray is tested against every other creature, and with 18 creatures vision is 14× the cost of the physics. So we timed a short training run from the 0.2 brain with 12 Terrikins in each of 32 arenas, 49k samples per update.

| | collecting | PPO update | samples a second |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 worker | ~1.0 s | ~5.3 s | ~7.7k |
| 4 workers | ~0.57 s | ~5.2 s | ~8.6k |

The update is 85–90% of the time, the same as solo 0.2. Speeding up rays would buy almost nothing, so it was dropped. The thing to speed up is the update (compile and bf16 halved it on a rented 3060, not tried on the Mac yet).

One cheap win turned up anyway, while adding nests to vision: the circle tests used `einsum`, which is slow on arrays this small. Doing x and y separately made each test about 45% faster with the same answers. Vision with 18 creatures at 64 arenas went from 103 to 118 ticks a second, even with nests added.

## Seeing home and nestmates (schema v2)

New inputs: the distance and direction to its own nest, a flag on any creature a ray hits saying whether it's a nestmate, and how far each ray gets before reaching its own nest or another colony's.

**Tried first, on paper: nests as a new kind of thing a ray hits.** Dropped before writing it:
- A nest would stop the ray, hiding everything past it, and a Terrikin standing on its nest would be blind.
- The 0.2 brain would see its own nest (solo worlds have one) as an unfamiliar kind and act differently, when the point of the schema is that old brains act the same until trained.

**What we did:** nests are flat ground. A ray reports reaching a nest as extra numbers on the ray, but only if it gets there before whatever it hits. New inputs start with zero weights, so the 0.2 brain scored exactly the same afterwards: 18.40 ± 0.14 solo, 10.65 / 166.0 s with 12 Terrikins.

## The crop and the store, first version

Food a Terrikin eats past full goes into its crop (2 at most), like an ant's social stomach, and slows it 15% for each. Unloaded in the nest, it's made into jelly in the store. Jelly gives back 0.25 energy against 0.4 for the food fresh.

**First version: unloading and eating were automatic.** Walk into the nest and the crop emptied; drop below the hunger line and it ate from its crop, or from the store at home. It worked, and a scripted carrier stored 10–13 food per creature, but it decided the behaviour for the creatures instead of letting them learn it. Unloading and eating were rewritten as actions. A Terrikin can unload anywhere: in a nest it makes jelly in that nest's store (a rival's too), and anywhere else it leaves the jelly on the ground for anyone to eat. In the first two runs below it left whole berries instead; jelly on the ground came after, since the store is meant to be jelly made from what they ate past full, not food carried about. Eating takes from its crop, or in its own nest from the store. Which of those pay off is up to the rewards (below). The one rule left is the hunger line on the store, as a dial to loosen later (a second, about the crop, came after the third run).

**Food a minute changed meaning.** A Terrikin that's full with a full crop now walks over food instead of eating it for nothing. 0.2 rewarded eating even when full, and the 0.2 brain is full most of the time, so its solo score fell from 18.40 to 5.23 food a minute, and greedy's from 13.79 to 5.04. That's the world changing, not the brain getting worse: they can't take more than they can eat or carry, so food a minute from before 0.3 can't be compared with it.

**Dropped food doesn't count as a meal.** Picking up food is rewarded, so if picking up dropped food counted too, dropping food and picking it straight back up would be an endless source of reward. Only food picked up where it grew counts. Unloading pays only into its own nest's store.

### A scripted carrier

Greedy with two additions: once its crop is full it walks straight home and unloads, and it eats its crop once it can without wasting any. It's the baseline a trained colony has to beat, the way greedy was for 0.2.

**First version starved.** With 3 colonies of 6, carriers lived 124 s on average. Of 137 deaths in 16 arenas, none was stuck or carrying anything. The median one died 13 m from home while its nest held 18 jelly. It filled the store, then foraged on, hungry, in ground it had already picked over. Going home to eat when hungry and the store has jelly fixed it: 166 s alive, 14 food stored each.

With actions instead of automatic feeding:

| | solo: food/min, seconds alive, stored | 3 colonies of 6 |
|---|---|---|
| random | 0.29, 49.1, 0 | 0.82, 61.4, 0 |
| greedy | 5.04, 180.0, 0 | 5.35, 172.9, 0 |
| carrier | 7.13, 174.7, 9.1 | 8.01, 169.4, 12.6 |
| 0.2 brain | 5.23, 180.0, 0 | 5.49, 163.6, 0 |

The 0.2 brain never unloads or eats from its crop: those actions are new to it and start at zero.

## Rewards

Half of each creature's reward is its own and half is its colony's average, so filling the store pays everyone a little and the one who carried it more. Dying is −1, each food unloaded into its own store +1, each jelly eaten from the store −0.5 (`configs/train.toml`). What food itself is worth changed after the first run.

### First colony run: a reward hack

5M samples from the 0.2 brain, 3 colonies of 6, gamma 0.997 (about 17 s ahead, since trips home take 8–12 s), about 10 minutes on the M3. Food paid 1 for every food picked up where it grew, eaten or carried.

| 3 colonies of 6 | food a minute | seconds alive | stored |
|---|---|---|---|
| carrier (scripted) | 8.01 | 169.4 | 12.6 |
| first colony brain | 9.66 | 179.3 | 0.2 |

Best at food and survival, and it stored almost nothing. It unloaded on 67% of ticks and carried something on only 6%. It had found that picking food up was what paid: grab a berry into the crop, drop it straight behind, grab the next. Dropped food doesn't count again, so it kept moving on to fresh food, and in one 3-minute episode 1,949 berries were dropped on the ground. The colony hoovered the arena and stored none of it.

There's a 28-second clip of it, landscape and portrait, kept with the stage's clips: one Terrikin dropping a berry every second or two, then the whole arena strewn with them.

The fix was to the reward, not a new rule: food now pays for the energy it actually gives when eaten (a whole food's worth when hungry, a quarter when nearly full, nothing for picking up), and storing pays when it's unloaded at home.

When a creature dies, its arena carries on. It stops counting in training until its arena starts over, and its life is one episode in the charts.

### Second run: no hack, no storing either

Same run with food paid for the energy it gives.

| 3 colonies of 6 | food a minute | seconds alive | stored | ticks carrying |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| carrier (scripted) | 8.01 | 169.4 | 12.6 | |
| first run (pickup paid) | 9.66 | 179.3 | 0.2 | 6% |
| second run (energy paid) | 10.32 | 140.6 | 0.0 | 0% |

The hoovering stopped, and so did any carrying: it chose to eat on 99% of ticks, so whatever went into its crop was eaten straight away, mostly while full. Storing pays well, but getting the reward once takes picking up food while full, carrying it for about 10 s, walking into the nest and unloading there. Exploring at random, that almost never happens, so the brain never finds out it pays. It also lived shorter than the first run, which isn't explained yet.

### Third run: paying the way home

Rewarding the store alone wasn't enough, since carrying never happened by chance. So the third run paid for the trip too: what's in a Terrikin's crop is worth 0.5 a food at its own nest, falling to nothing an arena's width away, and each tick it's paid the change in that worth (`[reward] home`). Walking home with a full crop pays, walking off costs, and eating or dropping the crop gives back what it was worth. This is potential-based shaping (Ng, Harada & Russell, 1999): what it pays along any path adds up to what it's worth at the end less what it was worth at the start, so it brings the store's pay forward without changing which way of living pays best.

The first way we wrote it was worth −distance from home while carrying. That was dropped before running it: eating the crop far from home would then have paid back the whole negative, which rewards exactly what the second run was already doing.

| 3 colonies of 6 | food a minute | seconds alive | stored | ticks carrying |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| second run (energy paid) | 10.32 | 140.6 | 0.0 | 0% |
| third run (way home paid) | 10.45 | 140.8 | 0.0 | 0.1% |

Nothing changed, and the reason was a step earlier than the shaping. The trained Terrikins are full most of the time (above 0.6 energy on 77% of ticks) and pick food up at a median of 0.89, so food does go into the crop. But they hold eat on all the time, and eating comes after picking up in the same tick: of 2,361 pickups made while full, 2,293 were eaten straight from the crop, wasting about 69% of each. Nothing was ever carried, so there was never a trip home to pay for. Eating while full only throws food away, but nothing tells the brain that, and holding eat on is the easiest thing to learn while being hungry pays.

The fix went into the body, not the reward: a Terrikin can't eat from its crop until there's room for a whole food, the same line that decides whether food it picks up goes to its stomach or its crop. Before, eating while full threw the extra away. Now the food stays in the crop, so a Terrikin that picks up food while full is carrying it whether it means to or not, and what's left for it to learn is where to take it and when to eat it.

### Fourth run: a packed lunch

| 3 colonies of 6 | food a minute | seconds alive | stored | ticks carrying |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| third run (way home paid) | 10.45 | 140.8 | 0.0 | 0.1% |
| fourth run (no eating past full) | 6.98 | 177.8 | 0.02 | 75% |

They carry now, and they live as long as the scripted carrier, but they keep the food for themselves. A Terrikin with a full crop doesn't head home (on average it drifts away at 0.05 m/s, a median 9 m out), and the few times one stood in its own nest with food in its crop, it unloaded on 1 tick in 655. It eats from its crop whenever it has room, and it's carrying more by the end of the run than at 3.7M samples.

That's the right answer to the rewards we gave it. Food eaten later from the crop pays the same 1.0 as food stored, with no walk home, and jelly from the store gives back 0.25 energy and costs 0.5 to eat. Nothing yet makes the store worth more to anyone than what's already in their crop. Food a minute fell because a Terrikin with a full stomach and a full crop walks over food, but it no longer starves: 5 of 288 had died at the one-minute mark against 48 in the third run. The shorter lives in the second and third runs went away with the waste, though how wasting food killed them isn't pinned down.

### Fifth run: paying more for something never tried

Storing was raised from 1 to 2 a food, so food taken home would be worth twice what eating it later from the crop is.

| 3 colonies of 6 | food a minute | seconds alive | stored | ticks carrying |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| fourth run (stored 1) | 6.98 | 177.8 | 0.02 | 75% |
| fifth run (stored 2) | 5.55 | 165.0 | 0.0 | 88% |

It never unloaded at all: 0 times in 753 ticks spent in its own nest with food in its crop. The price only matters if the brain ever gets it. Almost every unload it tried at the start of training was out in the open, where unloading gives up what the crop was worth, so it learned that unloading is bad before it ever unloaded at home. The way it starts, with an empty crop on a ring just outside its nest, never puts it in the nest with something to unload.

### Sixth run: starting at home with a full crop

If the brain never finds the nest with food to unload, start it there. A new world dial, `[crop] start`, fills every crop at the start of an episode (0 in the worlds we score in), and the sixth run's training world put the spawn ring inside the nest: each episode, every Terrikin starts at home, full, with unload still half-random. Unloading right there paid about +3 a creature on the first tick; holding on paid nothing.

Scored in the normal world: 5.52 food a minute, 170.3 s alive, stored 0.0. It never unloaded at home there, 0 times in 1,364 ticks home with a crop, and even at the start of its own training world its unload setting fell over the run (−0.19 at 3.7M samples, −0.42 at the end). The starts were too few to count: about 20 samples in every 74,000, against the third of all carrying ticks where an unload out in the open gave up what the crop was worth.

### Seventh run: shorter episodes, more starts

The same start, with 30-second episodes in training instead of 180, for about six times as many starts. It learned to unload at the start (its unload setting there went from −0.4 to +0.09), and nothing else carried over: in the normal world a Terrikin with a full crop still drifts away from home, and it spent 5 ticks in its own nest with food in its crop. Knowing that unloading at home pays doesn't help a brain that never gets there.

The pull home was weak. With the crop worth 0.5 a food at home, heading home instead of away was worth about 0.006 a tick, lost among the food rewards. Shaping guides best when the worth it uses is close to what something is really worth, and food in a crop at home is worth what storing it pays, 2.

### Eighth run: the crop worth what storing pays

`home` raised to 2, the same as storing, with everything else as in the seventh run. Nothing moved: stored 0.0 in the normal world, a Terrikin with a full crop still drifted away from home (0.03 m/s), and unloading fell further, since dropping a full crop in the open now cost up to 4.

Four times the pull home and no homing at all was the clue to look at the brain instead of the rewards. The inputs added in 0.3 start with zero weights, so older brains act the same. After 5M samples, the home sense's first-layer weights had grown to 0.06–0.13, against about 2 for the inputs the 0.2 brain already used, and the ray's nest distances to 0.01–0.03. The brain could barely tell where home was, so no reward for going there could be followed.

### A test with nothing else in it

To check that going home could be learned at all, a test world with no food and no blocks: every Terrikin starts 4 m from its nest with a full crop, in 30-second episodes, so going home and unloading are the only things that pay. From the 0.2 brain, 2M samples.

It didn't learn it. Reward fell to −0.022 a tick in the first updates and climbed back to 0 by the ninth, and that was all it learned: stop unloading. A new action starts at 0 with a spread of 1, so "unload" comes up yes on half of all ticks, and every Terrikin dumped its crop out in the open within its first few steps. None ever reached home still carrying anything, so unloading at home was never tried before unloading was learned as bad. Heading home while carrying ended at +0.01 m/s, and nothing was stored.

That's the trap behind the fourth to eighth runs too: an action that can't be taken back, tried at random every tick, gets judged everywhere but the one place it pays. The way out we're trying is a reverse curriculum: start where the right move is easy, then move the start further away a stage at a time, each stage training from the last one's brain.

### Reverse curriculum, first try

Three stages from the 0.2 brain, each starting from the last one's brain, in the test world with no food: start inside the nest (10-second episodes), then 2.5 m from its centre (0.9 m outside its edge), then 3.5 m (20-second episodes), 1M samples each.

| stage | reached home | stored, of 2 |
|---|---|---|
| start in the nest | 100% | 2.00 |
| 2.5 m out | 80% | 0.00 |
| 3.5 m out | 51% | 0.01 |

The first stage learned to unload, but not to unload *at home*: its unload setting was as high outside the nest as in it, so the second stage started with every Terrikin dumping its crop on the first tick (288 of 288), and 2M more samples didn't separate the two. The only clues it had to being home were the distance to its nest and the nest distances on its rays, small differences in numbers whose weights had barely grown.

So it gets a sense ants have: whether it's in its own nest, a self input that's 1 at home and 0 anywhere else (`at_home`). What it does there is still up to it.

### Second try: a sense of home, and a learning rate too gentle

With `at_home`, and 2M samples each for the stages outside the nest:

| stage | reached home | stored, of 2 | unload setting at home / outside |
|---|---|---|---|
| start in the nest | 100% | 2.00 | +0.12 / — |
| 2.5 m out | 85% | 0.08 | −0.11 / −0.11 |
| 3.5 m out | 50% | 0.19 | −0.19 / −0.24 |

Hardly better, and the unload setting was the same in the nest as out of it. The weights said why. Every part of the brain that's new in 0.3 had barely moved: the `at_home` input's weights had grown to 0.07, the home sense's to 0.03–0.09, the unload action's bias by under 0.01, and its spread was still exactly where it started, 1.0. Training uses Adam, which moves each weight by about the learning rate per step at most: 3e-4, falling to nothing over a run, across about 400 steps a million samples. That's about 0.06 a million samples, which is what they'd grown by. An action that starts at 0 with a spread of 1 needs its setting moved well past 1 before it's done on purpose rather than by chance. These were short runs on weights that start at zero, and the 0.2 brain was trained for 20M samples.

### Third try: a faster learning rate

The same three stages with the learning rate at 1e-3 instead of 3e-4. The first stage learned to unload again (stored 1.96). The second stored 0.10: 265 of 288 Terrikins still dumped their crop outside the nest, and the unload setting was −0.12 at home against −0.14 outside, so no split. The third stored 0.08, and Terrikins carrying a crop walked *away* from home at 0.5–0.75 m/s.

### A brain from scratch

To rule out the 0.2 brain's habits, a brand-new brain trained in the second stage's world (2.5 m out, full crop, no food) for 3M samples at 1e-3. It did worse than nothing: it dumped its crop on the first tick, 288 of 288, with its unload setting outside the nest up at +0.41. That looked like a bug, so we checked the reward for that tick by hand. Unloading on the first tick cost 3.6 a creature, exactly what the crop was worth there, and holding on cost 0.011 a tick. The rewards were right.

The shaping explains why dumping can still win. Under potential-based shaping, every way of living adds up to its true reward less what the crop was worth at the start. Dumping it now and carrying it forever without unloading come out exactly the same: only getting home *and* unloading there pays more. With `home` at 2, the same as storing, unloading at home paid nothing on the tick it happened either, because the walk home had already paid it. So the brain was choosing between two equal options, and small errors in its value guesses picked dumping.

### What was actually wrong: every new action started on a coin flip

All of the above points back at one thing. During training, actions are sampled around the brain's setting, and unload and eat count as yes above 0. A new action loaded into an older brain got a setting of 0 and a spread of 1 (its log spread was padded with 0), so it came up yes on half of all training ticks. An unload can't be taken back, so a crop never lasted the walk home, and unloading in the nest was almost never tried, however we paid for it. The evals take the setting itself, not a sample, so a brain at 0 never unloads there. That's why the docs could say a new action does nothing until it's trained. It was only true outside training.

So a new action now starts the way it does in a brand-new brain, with a spread of about 0.6. A yes/no one starts with its setting at −1, so it's yes on about 5% of samples and never in the evals. Nothing stops it unloading anywhere; it just isn't doing it half the time before it knows why. The same goes for a fresh brain.

### A grid on rented GPUs

Rather than one run at a time on the Mac, 12 runs at once on two rented A10s (Vast.ai), three or more side by side on each, 4M samples per run. That's 5–12 minutes a run and $0.56 in all, about half of it two pods sitting idle for 30–37 minutes after their last runs had finished, while the results were looked at. Two more pods failed to start and were dropped for $0.04. Every pairing of:

- the old start (setting 0, spread 1) or the new one (setting −1, spread 0.6),
- `home` at 0, 0.5 or 1, with storing paying 2,
- the second stage's test world (start 2.5 m out, full crop, no food) or the normal world.

What this taught us:

- Look at what exploring actually does with a new action before blaming the rewards. Five runs went into reward and curriculum changes when the problem was a coin flip at the start.
- An evaluation that uses only the setting hides learning that's under way. Sampling the way training does showed the split long before the evals did.
- Shaping can't help with a step that never happens. Once crops could get home, paying the way home did speed learning up.

### The dead leave the world

Until now a Terrikin that starved stopped where it was and its body stayed until the arena started over: others bumped into it, rays saw it as a creature, and it got pushed around. With colonies that's a corpse in the way, sometimes on the nest. Now a dead body touches nothing and nothing sees it; it stays in the frames where it fell so the viewer can put a small tombstone there, which fades after 20 s. Taking the body out, rather than drawing a stone over it, was what kept the picture honest, since a stone that fades over a body still in the way would hide something real. This changes training a little, so runs from here on have it and runs before don't.

The first replay with it (the 8M brain above, seed 2) shows something worth looking into: five of the blue colony starved on or beside their own nest about 30 s in.
