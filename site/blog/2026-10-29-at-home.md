SETI@home put spare computers to work looking for aliens. Folding@home put them to work on proteins. [Terrarium at Home](/at-home/) puts them to work teaching small clay blobs to bring food home before dark. The name's a joke, and so is the scale: it's mostly for a few friends with gaming PCs who asked if they could help. But it runs on other people's computers, so it had to be built properly anyway, and once it was, anyone with a spare GPU can ask for a token.

## Why more machines help

Every brain on this site learned by practice, and practice is samples: one Terrikin taking one step, over and over, a few billion times for a stage. So far that's been my MacBook Air and GPUs I rent by the hour, at about A$5.53 (US$3.88) a billion samples. That's cheap, but it adds up, and I keep the rented spend to a few dollars a day, so most experiments stop well short of a billion samples.

The bigger reason is that most of what's worked has come from trying several things at once, not from one long run. The brains that got colonies through the nights came from eight forks training side by side, every one scored after each round, the worst ones swapped for copies of the best with their settings nudged. Settling a dial, like how long an episode should be or how much food the world has, is the same: a fork for each value, side by side, see which does best. One GPU runs those one after another over a few nights. Ten idle GPUs run them in an evening.

So the work comes in jobs. A job is a chunk of training, say 8 million samples, which takes under an hour on most GPUs. When it's done the brain goes back to the queue, and the next chunk can start from it on whichever machine is free. A friend's laptop (Kelpie on the board) has already trained memory brains this way, a chunk at a time, while its owner was out.

Macs count too. An Apple silicon Mac trains on its own GPU, and my M3 MacBook Air does about 8,000 samples a second in a simple world and about 4,600 with three colonies. A rented RTX 3060 does 11,000 to 14,000 in the simple world. A Mac mini with an M4 Pro should be quicker than either, but nobody's lent me one to find out.

## What the bigger ideas cost

So far the whole project, three stages of it, has cost about A$40 (US$28.50) in rented GPUs. That's bought a lot of findings, and plenty of dead ends worth knowing about. But the things I'd most like to try look more like projects other people have already shown can work, and those were trained at a scale I can't rent by the hour.

The closest to Terrarium is OpenAI's [hide-and-seek](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.07528) from 2019: small agents in a physics world with boxes and ramps, one team hiding and one seeking, with nothing rewarding them for touching any of it. The hiders learned to build forts out of boxes and lock them in place, the seekers learned to use ramps to get over the walls, the hiders learned to put the ramps away first, and in the end the seekers learned to climb onto a box and ride it across the map. The paper says getting to the fourth of those stages, putting the ramps away, took 132.3 million games (31.7 billion frames) over 34 hours. Every frame moves two to six agents, so at what a sample has cost me on rented GPUs, that's US$250 to US$740 (A$350 to A$1,050) just to reach stage four, and box surfing turned up after 380 million games, nearly three times as many. Their world was full 3D physics and their brain was about six times the size of mine, so each of their steps costs more than one of mine: those numbers are the least it could be.

The other one I keep coming back to is DeepMind's [Capture the Flag](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.01281) agents, which learned to play a modified Quake III Arena two against two, seeing only what's on their screen, and got good enough that a team of two professional game testers won only a quarter of their games against them. Thirty agents trained side by side, each for two billion steps (about 450,000 games). That's 60 billion steps, at least US$230 (A$330) at my rate, and every one of their steps meant drawing a first-person picture for the brain to look at, which is far more work than a Terrikin's rays.

Those are one run each, and most of what's worked here has come from eight forks at once. So the ideas I'm most curious about are hundreds to thousands of dollars of rented time each. That's not much for a lab and a lot for a pet project, and it's most of why lent machines matter: a few idle gaming PCs over a few evenings would cover it.

## Built properly anyway

If someone's going to leave my program running on their computer, it should be something they'd be happy to leave running. It only takes jobs I've signed, and their copy of the code checks the signature, not my server. The code a job runs comes from this repo on GitHub, so anyone can read exactly what their machine would run. It waits until you've been away from the keyboard for 10 minutes, only works on mains power, uses at most half the CPU at low priority, and gives the job back the moment you come back, start a game or go full screen. It never sends a hostname, a user name or files. All of that is on the [At Home page](/at-home/), and what each rule does is in [docs/compute.md](https://github.com/MartianRiver/Terrarium/blob/HEAD/docs/compute.md).

It does use your power. A GPU working flat out for an hour uses about as much as a kettle in a few minutes of boiling, so it's cents, but it's your cents, and that's part of why it only runs on mains and never in the background on a laptop battery.

## The perks

If you're lending a machine, you get a few things the site doesn't have, because they need the sim and the brain running on a real computer.

**Watch what it trains.** `python -m terrarium.watch` opens the viewer on the brain your machine is training right now. Each time a replay ends it jumps to the newest checkpoint, so you see it get better while you watch, with the training charts on the runs page beside it.

**Play against it.** The Play button puts you in the world as one of the Terrikins, against the brain your machine is training. You score a point a second alive, 10 a berry picked up and 20 a food brought home, and the panel puts you beside the brain's worst, average and best. There's an energy bar, a sprint meter, your jelly belly filling up, and words that pop up when it's full or night's coming, since you can't feel hunger through a keyboard. It's harder than it looks: I tried it today and spent most of the game stressed out and starving, and that's with me seeing the whole garden while they only see what their rays show them.

**Look inside it.** Click a Terrikin to follow it, then press B for its brain window: what each of its rays sees, which of them it's paying attention to, how well it thinks things are going, and what it's about to do. N shows the whole brain wired up, with its units lighting up as it thinks.

All of it stays on your machine. A public version of play on the site would need the physics running in the browser, so it's not something I'm offering yet.

## Is anyone using it?

One friend's laptop so far, plus my own Mac. The [board](/at-home/#the-board) shows every machine that's trained anything and how much, next to the GPUs I've rented. If you've got an NVIDIA GPU or an Apple silicon Mac that sits idle a lot and you'd like to lend it, the At Home page says how to ask for a token.
