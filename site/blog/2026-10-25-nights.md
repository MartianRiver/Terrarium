This is where stage 0.3 is today. It isn't finished, but the colonies have learned the thing this stage was really about: getting through the night.

## Where it left off

After [the hoovering hack](/blog/hoovering/) I changed what food pays, and the hoovering stopped. So did any carrying. For a while nothing I tried got a crop of food home.

That turned out to be a bug, not the rewards. When a new action like "unload your crop" was added to an older brain, it started as a coin flip: it fired on half of all training ticks. A crop never lasted the walk home. Once a new action started rarely instead, they began carrying food home and storing it as jelly, and soon they were hoarders.

{{clip nights-piles}}

Then they starved right beside it. Of 267 Terrikins that starved in one round of tests, 262 died with jelly in their own colony's store, some of them sitting on the nest. Hungry, at home, with jelly right there, the brain never once chose to eat it.

<details>
<summary>Why they never ate the jelly</summary>

Eating jelly is another yes/no action, and a new one starts at "no" and gets tried on only about 5% of training ticks. Jelly was also priced below fresh food on purpose, so foraging would always pay better. Together that meant the brain almost never got to find out that a jelly beats starving. Paying more for jelly didn't change anything: a brain that never says yes never sees what yes pays.

</details>

## A world that needs a store

Stepping back, the real problem was the world. Food was everywhere, all the time. A script that never stores anything lived longer than one that carries food home, so nothing in the world gave a store a reason to exist. Animals store food for lean times, and there weren't any.

So I added night. One minute of every two is dark. The food left standing goes, nothing grows back until dawn, and the dark is cold: it drains energy, except in the nest, or a little less close to nestmates. A Terrikin can sense the light, and dusk fades slowly, so night can be seen coming.

To check the idea before training anything, I wrote a script called nester: it forages and carries like the others, heads home when the light starts to fade, and waits in the nest until morning. It got nearly every colony through the first night. A script that stays out after dark lost half to three quarters of them. Going home at dusk is the whole game.

## The first night brain froze

{{clip nights-first}}

The brain didn't know that. It stayed out, and all 18 froze.

I ran eight versions side by side on rented GPUs, each changing one thing. Every one learned a little, and none of them learned to go home at dusk. They kept dying in the dark beside a stocked store. The one that did best was the one that looked further ahead, which makes sense: going home at dusk only pays off by not dying half a minute later.

## What got them home

Two things.

**Paying for warmth.** I started paying a tiny bit for being warm, in the nest or close to nestmates, a little more in the dark. By day it's too small to stop them exploring. At night, with no food to be had, it's the only thing to earn. It pays the moment they're home, instead of only by not dying a minute later.

**Forks that manage themselves.** Instead of me picking which version to train on, a handful of brains train side by side, each with its own settings: how far ahead it looks, what warmth pays, how much it shares the colony's reward. Every few minutes the weakest ones copy the best ones' brain and settings, then nudge a few settings to try something new. This is called population-based training. It ran on my Mac and a few rented GPUs.

About 100 million samples later, a brain that never saw nester's script lives as long as nester does, loses fewer colonies, and forages more.

{{clip nights-now}}

At dusk everybody heads home. They huddle on the nest all night, and at first light they're off again.

<details>
<summary>The numbers</summary>

32 episodes of 6 minutes on seeds no training run uses, three colonies of six, a minute of night in every two:

| | seconds alive | colonies through the first night (of 96) | colonies lost by the end | food a minute |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| nester (script) | 303.7 | 96 | 11 | 3.01 |
| the brain | 307.2 | 91 | 7 | 4.30 |

The settings every brain in its line ended up with: looking about 100 seconds ahead, warmth in the dark paid a little, nothing paid for storing, and dying costing half a point.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why it isn't a copy of the script</summary>

I also tried starting a brain as a copy of nester and training on from there. It got home at dusk straight away and, after training, outlived nester by a lot. But it was shown the trip home, not something it found. The brain I'm carrying forward has only ever learned from rewards, and the copy stays what it should be: a benchmark to beat. Its worst episodes are still better than my brain's, and that's the gap to close.

</details>

## What's not right yet

They spin. All night, on the spot, flat out. Turning cost nothing, and nothing in the reward told spinning apart from sitting still. Turning costs a little energy now, by how hard they turn, like moving always has. Training with that has started.

And alone, this brain mostly dies. Put one in the garden by itself and it starves away from home in most episodes. It never trained alone. Worker ants are much the same: kept on their own they live far shorter lives, even with food to hand. So I'm not holding a lone Terrikin to what the Clover brain could do. What matters is that foragers away from the nest still keep themselves fed in the colony.

## Next

Less food than they need. Right now there's enough for everyone, so every colony on the same brain gets through about the same. With food for only about two colonies' worth, the colonies race each other for it, and whoever eats first gets through the night. Then the 0.3 release.
