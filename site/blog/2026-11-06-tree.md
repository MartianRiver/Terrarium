First, the creatures have a new name. They were Terrikins in the older posts; they're nims now, lowercase, like "ant": a nim, three nims. The name comes from "Nobody Instructs Me", which is the one rule here: nothing they do is scripted, they learn all of it.

The babies in [the nursery](/blog/nursery/) already had memory: in peekaboo they find a berry after it's gone out of sight, and they learn their way round small mazes. But all I could see there was the score. A brain with memory did better than one without, and that was as far as it went. Last week I got to look inside the memory of one of them, check what it holds, and change it to see what the nim does. It's a small thing in a small world, but for this project it's the clearest look yet at a nim remembering something and acting on it.

## The tree nursery

A friend watching the babies tidy up asked for a little tree whose fruit they'd have to jump for. So the tree nursery has one: apples hang just out of reach, a jump takes the one above you, and every jump costs a little, so jumping all the time doesn't pay. Then the apples had to be carried home. A nim holds one apple at a time and gets its point only once it's stored in the nest, so the nest started right beside the tree and moved away a step at a time. Each step is 8 million moves (one nim making one choice is one move), which takes 20 to 50 minutes on one of my own machines.

By the end of that the garden was 32 m across, the tree could be anywhere in a wide patch of it, and the nims could only see 4 m. They kept bringing apples home about as fast as a script that's told where the tree is. I thought that meant they were remembering where it was. It didn't. Wiping their memory every few seconds cost them almost nothing.

There were two reasons. The first was mine: I'd called the tree "out of sight" from the nest without measuring it, and at the sizes I'd said that about, it mostly wasn't. The second was the brains being clever. After storing an apple, a nim would stand on the edge of the nest on the side it came in, facing out, so the way back was already where it stood and which way it faced. It didn't need to remember anything.

So I gave the nest a burrow. A nim that stores an apple goes down into it and comes back up in the middle, turned any way at random. Now the way back is only in its head, if it's anywhere. The brains that had done so well fell to the level of a script that just searches.

## Learning to go back

Each nim's brain has notes: a set of numbers it carries from one moment to the next and learns what to write in. They learned to use them within one step of training in the burrow world. In the clip above, the same brain plays the same game twice. On the left it keeps its notes. On the right the notes are wiped every time it goes into the burrow, so it comes up knowing nothing about where it's been. The first trip is the same in both, since there's nothing to wipe yet. After that, the one that remembers walks back to a tree it can't see, again and again, and the wiped one wanders.

That's one game, so it's only a picture. Over 128 fresh games the brain brings home 5.3 apples a minute with its notes and 3.7 with them wiped. A script that's told where the tree is brings home 5.8.

## Is it really remembering the tree?

I asked GPT-6 Astra, through the Codex CLI, to attack the claim, and it had a fair point. Wiping the notes takes away more than where the tree is. It also takes the brain's sense of how far into a trip it is, and it gives the brain a blank start it never trained on, which could throw it off for reasons that have nothing to do with the tree. And the nim's senses carry a lot by themselves: a compass, the way home, the tree once it's close. So I put the nest in the middle of the garden, so the tree could lie any way from home and there'd be no usual direction to learn, and tested three brains three ways without training them any further.

**Swapping notes.** When a nim comes up from the burrow, I put in the notes from a different game, one where it came up at the same time facing the same way. If that game's tree was in the same place, it does about as well as with its own notes. If that game's tree was the other way, it's pulled toward that other tree, and it's slower home than with blank notes. Wrong notes are worse than none. With blank notes, it takes 4 to 8 seconds longer to get the next apple home than with its own.

**Reading them.** From the notes alone, a straight-line fit can say where the tree is to within about 1.5 m, and its direction to within 10 to 12°, even for trees in parts of the garden the fit never saw. From what the nim can sense at that moment, the same fit is 5 to 9 m out.

{{clip tree-moved}}

**Moving the tree.** This is the one I like watching. While the nim is down in the burrow, the tree jumps at least 6 m, somewhere it can't be seen from the nest. In the clip, the left half is the same game with the tree staying put. On the right the nim comes up and walks to where the tree was, gets within 20 cm of the empty spot, looks about there for a few seconds, then heads off and finds the tree in its new place. Over 512 games each, on the first trip after a move, the three brains walk to the empty old spot before seeing the new tree 32 to 39% of the time. With their notes wiped it's 14 to 20%.

## What it doesn't do

It doesn't notice that the tree has gone and work out where it went. Once it's moved, all three brains search for it like anything else would, and for two of them remembering costs about 4 seconds a trip, since they go to the old spot first. Nothing in this world has asked them to update a memory that's wrong.

It's also not as good as the script that's told where the tree is. In the middle-nest world the best brain brings home about 0.8 of what that script does. And all of this is one small world and three brains from one family, so I'd want to see it hold in a bigger world, with company, before saying much more.

I also took away the free first find. Until now each game started with the nim beside the tree, facing it, so memory only had to carry the trips after that. Without it, two of the three brains still find the tree in nearly every game, the third in three games out of four, and the trips back still run on memory. Another 8 million moves without the head start added a little, up to a quarter of an apple a minute.
