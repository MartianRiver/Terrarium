The colonies at the end of [0.3](/blog/colonies/) can get through three nights and hatch new Terrikins, but they still starve beside their own jelly, and they can't remember anything. Teaching either in the colony world is slow, because everything is happening at once: a brain that's trying to learn to remember is also trying not to die. So for 0.4 I built a nursery.

{{clip nursery-tag}}

## Why a nursery

A gardener raises seedlings in a nursery before planting them out, somewhere sheltered where one thing at a time can go right. Here it's a set of small worlds where the babies can't starve: they're young the whole game, so living costs them almost nothing, and there's no food to find. What's left is play, and the games children play are a good list of skills. Tag is chasing and getting away. Hide-and-seek is using what's in the way. Peekaboo is remembering something you can't see any more. Capture the flag is going somewhere on purpose and getting back. Mazes came after, as the classic test of memory.

The babies are the hatchling version of a Terrikin: smaller, slower, with big eyes and rosy cheeks, and young the whole game. Each world is a hedged lawn 9 to 20 m across with one to eight babies in it, and most games last a minute or a minute and a half. Ten times a second, every baby looks out through 17 rays fanned across the 200° in front of it, which tell it how far away the nearest wall, block, berry or other baby is along each one, and feels its own body: how it's moving and which side it's on. In tag it can hear too (more on that below). From that it picks how hard to push, which way to turn, and whether to sprint.

Each baby has its own copy of the same brain, and the points in each game add up to nothing between the sides, so one side only wins what the other loses. The brains are small. For tag and hide-and-seek, the brain attends over its 17 rays and its own senses at a width of 128: about 270,000 numbers. For peekaboo and mazes, which need memory, it's half that width, with 128 notes of memory (a GRU) that it carries from one moment to the next and learns what to write in: about 154,000 numbers.

The worlds are tiny, so a run is cheap. By the end of October the nursery had had about 2 billion practice moves (one baby making one choice is one move) over 111 runs on rented GPUs, mostly RTX 3060s at about US$0.09 an hour, for about A$7.65 (US$5.37) all up, plus a few runs on my MacBook. Most runs are 4 to 16 million moves and take under an hour. Peekaboo has had the most, about 740 million, then hide-and-seek and tag (about 340 million each), one long capture-the-flag run (300 million) and mazes (290 million).

## They find the holes first

What I didn't expect was how fast the games would test the rules rather than the babies. Every game so far has had a way to score that the rules allowed and I never meant, and a brain found each one within a few million samples. It's the same lesson as [the hoovering](/blog/hoovering/) in 0.3, where food paid when it was picked up, so they picked it up and dropped it again: a brain does exactly what the world pays for, and a game is only as good as its rules. In the nursery that lesson comes round every day or so.

{{clip nursery-tagbacks}}

**Tag.** Being tagged meant 2 seconds before you could pass it back. Two babies learned to stand touching and pass it back and forth the moment the wait ran out: 29 tags a minute, the most the rule allows. So tag got the playground rule, no tag-backs. Retrained under it, a brain that started knowing nothing learned real chasing and getting away, and catches about twice as often as the 0.3 brain does.

{{clip nursery-wall}}

**Peekaboo.** A berry turns up in front of the baby for a second, then goes out of sight but stays where it is. If the spot in front was outside the arena, the code pulled the berry back inside. One brain learned to park against a wall, facing it, so every berry landed half a metre from its nose. It found 99% of them, better than a baby that's always told where the berry is. Now a berry that won't fit in front goes anywhere on open ground.

{{clip nursery-lap}}

**Peekaboo, again.** To make memory matter, the baby is spun round as the berry hides, so walking straight on isn't enough. Within 2M samples, a brain with no memory at all learned to run a fixed lap of the arena at full speed. The next berry always turned up ahead of it, so on its lap, and after the spin it just found its lap again and ran into the berry: 60% found. So now it's spun round as the berry turns up as well. Then a review found that the spin turned the body but not the way it was sliding, and a script that turned to face its own drift found 43% with no memory. The drift turns with the body now too.

{{clip nursery-blindspot}}

**Tag among blocks.** This one I only spotted by watching. A Terrikin sees 200° in front, and the runners learned to get in close behind whoever's it and stay there as it turns, the two of them going round and round in a tight circle. Someone sat behind the it, out of view, for 31% of the game.

{{clip nursery-ears}}

The fix wasn't a rule this time. It was a sense: Terrikins can hear now, the nearest other one within 3 m, all the way round and through blocks. I trained three brains with hearing and three without, from the same start. With it, someone sits in the blind spot 12% of the game; without it, 21%. Runners that can hear also get away better. That gap is small enough that I'd want more seeds before calling it settled, but every hearing seed came out below every deaf one.

Not all of them were holes. Some were just the easy way round a game. In tag and hide-and-seek, the 0.3 brain learned to run and hide and forgot how to chase and seek, and once both sides only hide, nobody's ever found, so there's nothing left to learn from. In capture the flag the defenders learned to camp in their own base, where every pickup happens. When I changed the rules so a touch steals the flag off its carrier, 300M samples of self-play learned not to pick a flag up at all, since taking one mostly hands the other side a robbery. Playing against a pool of their own past selves helped one seed of two pick flags up again, in 93% of games, but nobody has carried one home yet, and paying them for carrying it homeward taught one brain to keep goal instead.

Every one of these is saved, with its replay and the brain that found it, before it was fixed. OpenAI's hide-and-seek agents surfed on boxes and threw ramps out of the map. Ours park against walls. It's the same thing at a smaller size, and I want them all kept so I can show them together later.

## What they learned once the holes were shut

{{clip nursery-seek}}

Hide-and-seek with a fuller fan of sight (17 rays where a berry straight ahead used to slip between 16) is the first time seeking has been learned at all: two of three brains learned both halves, and the best one's seekers have a hider in sight 86% of the time after the count. Its hiders haven't kept up, though. Seeking pays all game, while hiding only teaches by being found, so there's no arms race yet.

{{clip nursery-peek}}

Peekaboo is where memory has started to show. The trick that worked was steps: the hidden berry flashes back briefly every 2 seconds, then every 4, then every 8, then never, so the memory has to hold the spot a little longer at each step. Two brains trained that way find 7.8 and 7.0 berries a minute, against 3.9 and 1.9 for two that only ever got flashes every 3 seconds, and wiping their notes every 3 seconds cuts them by a third to a half, so they're really using what they remember. A baby with a perfect memory finds about 12.

Mazes are earlier. A baby that keeps a wall on its left finds 0.6 berries a minute in a 5×5 maze, and one that knows the maze finds 5.6. Our best so far finds 2.15, which is better than following walls but a long way from knowing the way. That gap is what memory has to close, and the same skill is what finding food and getting home will need.

{{clip nursery-tidy}}

The newest game is a tidy one. Terrikins can leave droppings now, and being near one makes you a little sick, so a colony will need somewhere to put them. In the tidy nursery a baby gets a point for each dropping it takes past a dashed line outside the nest. A brain that started knowing nothing learned it in 4 million samples on my MacBook: it picks one up, sets it down a little further on, picks it up again, and takes 43 out in a game where a baby moving at random takes none. Moving the line out worked in steps, the same way peekaboo's did. The brain that learned at 1 m takes 8.9 out a game when the line is 4 m away, and after steps at 2.5 m and 4 m it takes 16.4. In the colonies themselves, nobody's made a midden yet: the droppings drift back towards home.

## What's next

The main line hasn't moved on yet. The best young-colony brain, a fresh one with memory, ends with 7.24 Terrikins of a possible eight against the 0.3 brain's 6.29, but that's one seed of two, and the other never learned to live. The plan is to take what the nursery teaches back into the colony world: memory first, in steps like peekaboo's, then the mazes as a way home.

The nursery's nights also turned up something about the main line. Another 32 million samples of plain self-play makes the 0.3 brain eat more and end with smaller colonies, 5.9 Terrikins where it had 6.36. What it's paid for (food eaten and stored) has drifted from what I actually want, colonies that last, so that gets fixed before any more colony training.

The nursery is also where I test training itself, since a mistake there costs cents. The latest: going over each batch of practice twice instead of four times learned as well or better in all three seeds, for about 60% of the time spent learning. That's only a hint so far, from one game with brains that have no memory, but if it holds it makes every run cheaper.

And to keep watching. Most of these holes showed up in the numbers, but the blind spot only showed up because I was watching a replay and something looked odd. The nursery channel on the [watch page](/watch/) plays the newest of each game as it trains, so you can watch for the next one too.
